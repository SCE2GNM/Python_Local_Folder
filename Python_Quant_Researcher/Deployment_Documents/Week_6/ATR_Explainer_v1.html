ATR — Average True Range Wilder 1978

Indicator Reference · Strategy Type: Volatility Measurement · Not a signal — a ruler

Strategy Type: Reference / Educational  ·  Timeframe: Any  ·  Used in: ETH ADX trailing stop research

📐 Indicator Explainer

1 — What ATR Measures

Average True Range (ATR) is a volatility indicator. It measures how much an asset's price moves on a typical day — in dollar terms. A higher ATR means larger daily price swings; a lower ATR means quieter, smaller moves.

ATR does not tell you which direction the market is moving. It has no bullish or bearish signal. It is purely a measure of how much price is moving, not where it is going.

ATR was created by J. Welles Wilder Jr. and published in his 1978 book New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems — the same book that introduced the ADX, RSI, and Parabolic SAR. All four indicators share the same underlying building block: the True Range.

Wilder created ATR to solve a specific problem: simple High–Low range ignores overnight gaps. If a stock closes at $100, gaps up overnight to $115, then trades between $115–$120, the simple range is only $5 — but the actual price movement experienced by a trader was $20. True Range captures this.

Key intuition: ATR is a volatility ruler. It tells you how much ETH is moving per day in dollar terms, not where it's going. A high ATR means wide swings — stops need to be wider. A low ATR means tight consolidation — tighter stops are appropriate.

2 — Step-by-Step Calculation

Step 1 — True Range (TR)

True Range is the largest of three possible ranges for each bar. The three candidates account for intraday movement, gaps up from prior close, and gaps down from prior close:

True Range = max of three values: TR = max(
    High − Low, ← standard intraday range
    |High − Previous Close|, ← gap up overnight
    |Low − Previous Close| ← gap down overnight
)
Why three candidates? On a normal day with no gap, H−L is largest. But on a day that gaps up above yesterday's close, the full true range must include that gap — the trader who held overnight experienced that movement. The absolute values ensure both gap-up and gap-down scenarios produce positive ranges.
The Three TR Scenarios — Visual Guide
A — Normal Day
No overnight gap · H−L wins
TR = H − L
B — Gap Up
Opened above PrevClose · |H−PrevC| wins
TR = |H − PrevClose|
C — Gap Down
Opened below PrevClose · |L−PrevC| wins
TR = |L − PrevClose|

Step 2 — Wilder's Smoothing (ATR)

ATR is the smoothed average of True Range using Wilder's own exponential smoothing method. This is the same smoothing Wilder used for RSI and ADX — it gives more weight to recent values but never drops older values completely.

First ATR (after N bars): ATR₁ = (TR₁ + TR₂ + ... + TRₙ) / N  ← simple average of first N true ranges

All subsequent bars: ATRᵢ = ( (N − 1) × ATRᵢ₋₁ + TRᵢ ) / N  ← Wilder's smoothing (equivalent to EWM with α = 1/N)

Common period: N = 14 (Wilder's original recommendation, matches RSI period). Our ETH ADX bot research used N = 9 (same as ADX window) for the ATR trailing stop tests.

Wilder's smoothing vs simple moving average: A 14-period SMA drops the oldest bar completely each day. Wilder's smoothing never fully forgets old data — it decays exponentially. This produces a smoother ATR that is less reactive to a single extreme day, which is desirable for a volatility measure used in stop placement.

3 — Worked Example (ETH, 3-Day ATR)

Using 3 days of realistic ETH price data. ATR(3) — the 3-day ATR — uses the simple average of the first 3 True Ranges as the initial value.

Day Prev Close High Low Close H − L |H − PrevC| |L − PrevC| TR = max
Reference — — — $2,380 — — — —
Day 1 $2,380 $2,450 $2,310 $2,400 $140 $70 $70 $140
Day 2 $2,400 $2,440 $2,370 $2,420 $70 $40 $30 $70
Day 3 $2,420 $2,500 $2,400 $2,478 $100 $80 $20 $100
ATR(3) = ($140 + $70 + $100) / 3 = $103.33

Yellow cells = largest of the three TR candidates (the winning value). H−L wins on all three days — no overnight gaps in this example. If a gap occurred, one of the other columns would be yellow instead.

Reading the result: ATR = $103.33 means ETH is moving roughly $103 per day on average over the past 3 days. At an ETH price of ~$2,450, that is approximately 4.2% daily volatility. A stop placed 2× ATR below entry ($206.67) sits far enough to avoid being hit by normal daily noise.

4 — What ATR Tells You

High ATR — price is making large moves. This typically occurs during:

  • Bear market crashes (large down moves)
  • Strong trend acceleration (large up moves)
  • News events and liquidation cascades

Low ATR — price is moving in a tight range. Typical in:

  • Sideways consolidation before a breakout
  • Low-volume weekend or holiday periods
  • Post-crash mean reversion to range

ATR vs percentage volatility

ATR is in dollar terms — useful for position sizing and stop placement in absolute dollar amounts. Percentage-based volatility (e.g. daily return standard deviation × 100) is relative. Both are valid; ATR is preferred in position sizing because real risk is dollar-denominated, not percentage-denominated.

ATR has no directional signal

A rising ATR during a downtrend and a rising ATR during an uptrend look identical. Always pair ATR with a directional indicator (e.g. ADX for trend strength, RSI for mean reversion level) when making entry decisions.

Key intuition: Think of ATR as the market's heartbeat — it tells you how fast and hard the heart is beating, not whether the patient is healthy or sick. Wide ATR = racing heartbeat. Narrow ATR = resting. Neither is inherently good or bad — it depends on your strategy and timeframe.

5 — How ATR Is Used in Trading

📏 Position Sizing

Set a fixed dollar risk per trade, then divide by ATR to get position size. This automatically reduces size during volatile periods and increases it during calm periods — keeping risk per trade constant.

Risk per trade: $100
Stop distance: 2× ATR
   = 2 × $103.33 = $206.67
Position size:
   = $100 / $206.67
   = 0.484 ETH
   ≈ $1,198 notional

🛑 Stop Placement

Place the stop N × ATR below entry. This adapts to current market conditions — wider stop when volatile, tighter stop when calm. Common multipliers: 1.5×, 2×, 2.5×. The stop moves up with peak price (trailing) using the same logic.

Entry: $2,478
ATR: $103.33
Stop (2× ATR):
   = $2,478 − (2 × $103.33)
   = $2,478 − $206.67
   = $2,271.33
   = 8.3% below entry

🔍 Regime Filter

Compare current ATR to its rolling average. A rising ATR often precedes or accompanies trend moves (expansion phase). A falling or low ATR signals consolidation. Some strategies only enter when ATR is above its N-day average, filtering out low-volatility chop.

ATR today: $103
ATR 20-day avg: $82
Ratio: 1.26×
→ ATR expanding
→ Trend environment
→ Trend entry valid
Position sizing with ATR is the correct approach when risk is dollar-denominated. A fixed percentage stop (e.g. 8%) gives a different dollar risk depending on how big your position is, not how volatile the market is. ATR-based sizing keeps your actual dollar risk constant regardless of entry price or current volatility — critical for portfolio-level risk management as positions scale up.

6 — How ATR Relates to ADX

Both ATR and ADX were created by Wilder in 1978. Both are built on True Range as the foundational calculation. The divergence is in what they do with it:

Property ATR ADX
Creator / year J. W. Wilder, 1978 J. W. Wilder, 1978
Foundation True Range (TR) True Range (TR) + Directional Movement
What TR is used for Smoothed directly → volatility measure Normalises DI+ and DI− → trend strength
Output Dollar value (e.g. $103) 0–100 score (e.g. ADX = 25)
Answers the question How much is price moving? Is there a trend? How strong?
Directional? No — volatility only No — strength only (DI+/DI− gives direction)
Smoothing method Wilder's smoothing (α = 1/N) Wilder's smoothing (α = 1/N)
Typical period 14 (Wilder) or 9 (our bot) 14 (Wilder) or 9 (our bot)
Used together? Yes — ATR for stop distance / position sizing; ADX for entry signal. Our ETH ADX bot tested ATR trailing stop (2.5× ATR) combined with ADX 19/9 entry signal.
Memory aid: ADX answers "IS there a trend?" — it measures directional conviction. ATR answers "HOW BIG are the moves?" — it measures move magnitude. A market can have high ADX (strong trend) with low ATR (small steady moves) or low ADX (no trend) with high ATR (large chaotic swings). Use both together: ADX for when to enter, ATR for how much to risk.

Why Percentage Trail Won on ETH Daily Candles

ATR advantage: Adaptive to volatility changes — stop widens automatically in volatile periods and tightens in calm periods. Theoretically superior for assets with regime-switching volatility. Also produced a lower MtM MaxDD (−27.8% vs −36.9%), meaning smaller peak-to-trough drawdowns during the backtest.

Why pct trail won overall: On ETH daily candles, ATR variability introduced stop placement noise that reduced captured trend gains. ETH's ATR fluctuates significantly day to day — a 2.5× ATR stop moved inconsistently, sometimes exiting positions at natural volatility spikes before the trend completed. The fixed 8% percentage stop provided a more stable, predictable stop distance that allowed trends to develop fully. The result: +6.3pp more annual return from riding more of each trend, at the cost of accepting somewhat larger drawdowns (+9.1pp deeper MaxDD).

When ATR would be preferred: On shorter timeframes (hourly, 4H) where volatility clustering is more pronounced and an adaptive stop provides genuine protection against intraday spikes. Also appropriate for multi-asset portfolios where normalising stop distance across assets with different volatility profiles is required.

Decision: Percentage trailing stop (8%) deployed in the live ETH ADX bot. ATR trailing stop remains a valid alternative and should be revisited if the strategy is adapted to shorter timeframes or applied to assets with more extreme volatility differences across regimes. Both methods outperform the fixed 5% stop on all risk-adjusted metrics.
